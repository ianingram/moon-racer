THE REAL THING

Everything in this manual happens here, in real places, at their real size.

THE RACE

Moon Racer is a tour of the world's ancient megalithic sites. A race starts at Giza. Each leg takes you from one site to the next by whatever route you choose — up into orbit along a lane, or low over rivers, oceans and ice — into a run through real terrain, and it ends only when you land at a pyramid.

Leg one: Giza → Hells Canyon. Take the lane 11,041 km round the curve of the Earth, come down at the canyon's entry by Hells Canyon Dam, run the Snake River north through the gates, and after the last gate at the Grande Ronde, find the beacon on Craig Mountain and land.

A leg is never finished at a checkpoint. The clock stops when you are down inside the ring at the pyramid.

Fuel, heat and the race clock carry from one part of the leg to the next. What you arrive with from the lane is what you start the canyon with.

THE ENGINES · FUEL

The craft runs on water. The air engines drink it from whatever is around them and burn it for thrust. The tank holds 10 kg.

Thrust and speed burn fuel. Empty, the engines stop: in the air you glide and sink; in space you coast.

Drinking is not free. Water through the intakes runs the engines hotter — drink with the throttle eased back.

THE DECK

500 ft over the water is the deck: the intakes draw water and humid air at full rate, clear of the rocks, trees and debris on the river. There is no need to go lower. The deck is soft — you can fly below it — but below about 300 ft, fast, the river throws up debris that costs speed and heat, and the copilot will call you back up. The gold gates hang at deck height.

HEAT

Heat comes from what the engines burn and tear through around the craft. Driving hard through thick air rips its molecules apart — hydrogen and oxygen from the water, nitrogen and oxygen from the air — and the space round the craft overheats.

  • Heat rises with speed squared times how thick the air is, and again with throttle times how rich the intake is (water richest).
  • In a vacuum there is nothing to burn: no heat is made at any speed.
  • At 100% the engines cut out until heat falls to 55%. Push past that to 120% and they break down, and stay out until 30%. Heat can never go past 150%.

WHERE HEAT WILL SETTLE

At any speed and height, heat heads towards one level. The green tick on the heat bar shows it. If the tick is past the end of the bar, you are heading for a cut-out — ease off, climb, or vent.

In the canyon a steady cruise at about 20–25% throttle holds just under the limit. In the thin air between about 10 and 30 km heat is still made but hardly shed: open up only once you are nearly out of the air.

THE VORTEX, THE COLD CORE & ICE

SHEDDING HEAT

Heat leaves the craft through its own wake vortex: the low-pressure core cools by expansion and the high-pressure ring carries the heat out and away. So shedding needs air — the more of it and the colder, the better. In a vacuum only a slow glow of radiation is left, so heat you carry up, you keep.

VENTING

Hold SPACE to spin the vortex up: it sheds three times as fast, but its drag costs speed. You see two counter-rotating spirals behind the craft, and a swirl on the water when you are low over it. In a vacuum venting does almost nothing.

THE COLD CORE

Like a vortex tube, the spun-up vortex throws its hot rim off and its core goes colder the longer and harder it spins. Venting banks cold (about 8 seconds to full at river level). Stop venting and the banked cold soaks up engine heat until it is spent — vent in a calm stretch, then run hard through the next on the cold you saved.

ICE — THE OTHER EDGE

Drive the core past two-thirds and ice forms on the intakes and hull — fast in wet air (river spray, cloud, rain), slowly in dry. Ice takes up to 60% of your power and weighs the craft down; the hull whitens with frost. Full ice chokes the intakes and the engines stop until it melts back to 40%. Heat melts ice: stop venting, leave the wet air, or run warmer.

JUMPING THE BENDS

Two skills win a run: flying the river, and jumping it.

FLOOD AND JUMP

  • Get down to the deck over the water and hold J. The engines flood: the JUMP gauge fills (2.5 s to full), water is drawn from the tank, and the heat climbs. While you flood, the engines won't cut out — the heat is going into the charge.
  • Let go. The energy dumps into the river as a steam blast that throws the craft up and on in a long arc, over the ridge between two bends. The heat goes into the water with it: you come out cool, and fast.
  • A bigger charge flies higher and further. A full charge reaches about 2 km up and 16 km on. In the arc you can steer, but not climb or throttle.
  • Come down on the water and it is a splashdown. Come down on a ridge or wall and it hurts like any wall.

JUMP POINTS AND JUMP GATES

Jump points are where the river swings round a bend far enough, and the ridge inside it is low enough, for a jump to cut it: 8–15 km of flight that saves 2–3 km of river. At each one a cyan jump gate hangs at the top of the right arc. Fly through it and the gold gates on the bend you jumped are cleared with no penalty. Jump without taking it and those gates count as missed.

The first stretch below the dam is river only — it is where the line is learnt. At every jump point the probe jumps first, a few seconds ahead of you: watch its vapour trace the arc through the cyan gate, then follow. It also marks the river line round the bend, more faintly, so both ways are shown. The first two jump points also show their arc as a faint cyan line.

Neither line always wins. Jumping is faster but costs water; the river is slower but you drink all the way. The best runs mix the two.

THE STARDUST ENGINE

Everywhere inside the atmosphere the stardust engine is IDLE. Past the Kármán line — 100 km, the edge of space — it becomes ACTIVE and takes over from the air engines, on the same water, at an efficiency nothing in the air can match:

One kilogram of water carries the craft to the Moon — 384,400 km — in a day. Going faster costs more, as the square of the speed.

In space there is no air to slow you. Pull the throttle back and the engines brake, which burns fuel. A 300 g reserve is held back that only the brakes can use, so a dry tank can still stop — but plan to arrive with fuel.

NAVIGATION

LANES

Any two places on Earth lie on one great circle. Lifted into orbit it becomes a lane: a ring that passes straight over both ends, so it is always overhead from either end even when the destination is far below the horizon. Giza → Hells Canyon: bearing 338° from Giza, tilted 70.8°, over Poland, the Greenland Sea and Hudson Bay, 2,000 km up. Hells Canyon rises over the horizon about 4,400 km out.

FLYING THE LANE

  • Hold ↑ to climb out. The craft lifts itself off for the first seconds; after that, climbing is yours.
  • Below the lane you are held to 60% of full speed; in it you have everything.
  • About 1,300 km out, pull the throttle right back and brake; then dive. You arrive within 40 km of the canyon entry, below 25 km. Overshoot and it costs 30 seconds.

RECON, BEFORE THE RUN

At the start of a run you can choose RECON: the pathfinder leads you over the whole course about 1,500 ft above the high ground, with the canyon, the gates, the jump points and the landing field laid out below, and the copilot pointing each out. Nothing is timed; fuel and heat are held. Practise the controls up there — the race itself is flown down in the canyon, below. Enter starts the run at any time.

RUNS

A run follows real ground — here the Snake River through Hells Canyon, the deepest river gorge in North America. Gates are gold rings over the water; missing one costs 3 s, clipping a wall 2 s and a jolt of heat.

THE PATHFINDER — THE FORWARD PROBE

A companion probe that flies about 4 km ahead at your pace, on the deck. It vents compressed air and water from its tanks, and its vapour trail is the route: fresh and bright ahead of you, rising, drifting and thinning once you pass. At each jump point it jumps first, to show you the arc. If you are off course, going the wrong way, stalled, low on fuel, broken down or iced, it comes back, circles you, draws a dashed line to the nearest water, and if the tank is low and water far, calls in rain.

THE MAPS

The globe shows the planet dark with its coasts, the axis from pole to pole, the Arctic and Antarctic Circles, the lane and the sites. The route map in a run shows the river, the gates, the pathfinder, the landing field and you.

LANDING

Every landing field is the same: a pyramid with its beacon lit — a shaft of light you can see from far off — inside a gold ring 2.4 km across, six runways running in, and six stands. Fields stand on flat plateaus, never on a site itself.

  • After the last gate, follow the beacon (and the gold dashed line) to the field.
  • Come in slowly and set down inside the ring below about 90 speed.
  • Landing on a field costs nothing; the pyramid itself is solid.
  • Landing before the last gate does not count — the copilot will tell you.

THE COPILOT

The copilot speaks through the pathfinder. It watches the craft and calls out what matters, one thing at a time: heat heading past the limit, fuel low with water near, ice forming, the stardust engine coming alive, time to brake, where to land. It speaks aloud (turn the voice off with V) and writes on the screen.

Press / to ask it something — "why do my engines cut out?", "how do I refuel?", "where do I land?" — and it answers from this manual.
